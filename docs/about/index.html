The short version

It started with a medical device

NorviTech was founded in 2018 to take the guesswork out of nutrition and hydration intake monitoring in hospitals and care homes: a weight-based device that records what a patient actually ate and drank, and hands the numbers to the care-management system, instead of a clinician estimating a tray by eye. The design was shaped by consultation with more than seventy-five clinicians and administrators around the world, taken from idea to working prototype by a multidisciplinary team, and funded by a British government grant and academic partnerships. I am a co-inventor on the United States patent that came out of it, granted in 2024.

Then eight years of healthcare data

Before and since the device I have spent eight years turning clinical and financial data into decisions: clinical-trial and patient-chart analytics at a regional health system, then four years of post-acute and value-based care analytics — more than two hundred and fifty revenue cycle reports, and the enterprise data models behind them.

Today I own the Snowflake semantic model behind revenue cycle reporting at a health technology company: a modular model with an enforced record grain, automated parity testing against the legacy model it replaces, and a staged retirement of that model gated on evidence rather than a date. Alongside it, a shared hosting platform that runs sixteen internal applications on AWS from one application registry, with single sign-on, immutable image deployments and automated per-site health checks; agent-based automation that triages billing errors, maps payors, categorises denials and prepares rebills; and a code reviewer, backed by a large language model, wired into merge-request workflows.

Today it is the name I publish software under

The same instincts carried over — measure rather than guess, keep the data where it belongs, make the whole thing readable end to end — and they are the house rules of the NorviTech Suite: no telemetry, no accounts, no hosted service. Each README says what leaves the machine, and each release is a tag you can read in full.

What I work on

Helios

A native GTK4 desktop for coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI Codex and OpenRouter — with permissions, budgets and plans where you can see them.

BitAgent

A self-hosted DHT crawler and indexer for the *arr stack that learns from what your library actually keeps. A fork of bitmagnet, credited as such.

XNote and XNote Placement

Sticky notes rebuilt for GNOME on Wayland, with encrypted backup, and the Shell extension that puts each note back where it was.

SnipSnap

Screenshots on GNOME Wayland without the permission dialog, with the selection drawn inside the compositor. A fork of Flameshot.

How I work

Measure the mechanism

A fix is not done when the symptom goes away; it is done when the instrument that would have caught it reads non-zero. Every app ships with the metrics or the test that proves its own claim.

Prove the replacement before you retire the original

The habit comes from the day job, where a new data model is not trusted because it was built carefully but because it was tested line for line against the one it replaces. Nothing here is cut over on a date; it is cut over on evidence.

Release mirrors, not moving targets

Development happens in a private tree; every tagged release is exported through an automated gate that refuses anything that should not be public. What you clone is exactly what was reviewed.

Forks stay honest

Three of the five apps descend from someone else's work. They keep the upstream licence, notices and authors, and say plainly what is upstream's and what is new.

Your data stays yours

I run all of this on my own machines, so the question "what leaves the box?" gets asked first, and answered in each README.

Automate the toil, don't absorb it

Helios exists because I spend my working day directing coding agents and wanted the permissions, the spend and the plan somewhere I could see them. Every app here started as my own repeated annoyance.
